What Stanford’s Payroll Data Say About Young U.S. Software Developers

PUBLISHED 2026.11.23 14 MIN READ — VIEWS #Software Developers#AI#Employment#Stanford#ADP

Editorial summary

The 38.8 percentage-point gap is reproducible from an earlier Stanford release. In the October 2026 release from Stanford Digital Economy Lab and ADP Research, September headcount for software developers aged 22–25 was 18.56% below its November 2022 level. Headcount for ages 41–49 was 20.20% above it. The November release revised September and added October. It shows changes of −16.85% and +20.80%, a gap of 37.65 percentage points. The gap is wider than a year earlier and below its May 2026 peak. These payroll records show a substantial age divergence in the sample. The series alone cannot predict whether it will widen or estimate how much of it AI caused. Stanford Canaries Dashboard

November 23 update. Worker-flow studies point to fewer hires. One study using actual GitHub Copilot licensing isolates an adoption effect for a group of large firms. The share of the national decline among young developers attributable to AI remains unmeasured. A new estimate from linked CPS interviews finds fewer reported main-employer changes among young developers in the later pooled period and too few job-loss events to establish a layoff trend. The season-matched 2026 point estimate is higher, and the year-over-year confidence interval still includes zero.

Where the 38.8 points came from

The claim accompanying the WeChat link cites changes of −18.6% and +20.2% and a gap of 38.8 percentage points. The link returned an access challenge during verification. We checked the supplied figures against Stanford's original downloads.

The dashboard's Software Developer Employment Index in Canaries Sample by Age Group sets each group’s own November 2022 headcount to 100. We checked the baseline and the September and October observations in canaries_software_developers.csv inside each dated ZIP. Subtract 100 from the index to get the cumulative percent change for that age group. Subtract the younger group's change from the older group's change to get the gap in percentage points. The original 38.76 rounds to 38.8. Two things account for the 1.11-point difference between the earlier September estimate and the latest October estimate. October is the last observation available when checked on November 23, 2026.

The gap remains large and has narrowed since May

Within the November release, the gap rose from 24.32 percentage points in October 2024 to 34.45 in October 2025. It reached 40.63 in May 2026 and eased to 37.65 by October, still 3.20 points wider than a year earlier. It fluctuated in the intervening months. The series does not establish where it will go next. November release ZIP

The year-over-year series provides a shorter view. In October 2026, developer headcount was down 0.12% for ages 22–25 and up 2.63% for ages 41–49, a difference of about 2.75 percentage points in annual growth. The youngest group remained well below its 2022 baseline while its latest annual change was close to flat. In the same month, ages 26–30 were down 4.72% from November 2022. Ages 31–34, 35–40 and 50+ were up 7.00%, 12.70% and 8.00%, respectively. November release ZIP, which includes the year-over-year CSV

What the payroll sample can establish

The dashboard rolls forward a five-year panel of firms using ADP and includes workers successfully matched to an occupation code. This helps track employment inside firms that remain in the panel and limits coverage of firm entry, exit, payroll-provider changes and employment reallocation across firms. The researchers say the panel represents neither the entire U.S. labor market nor all ADP clients. People can age into and out of the bands, and the groups have different starting headcounts. The index measures each group’s employment relative to its own baseline. Layoffs, new hires, unemployment and openings for junior positions require their own counts or denominators. Dashboard methodology

Estimating how much AI affected the age gap requires evidence about employer adoption, the tasks that changed and a credible comparison group. An occupational exposure score measures the potential for AI to affect tasks. In a February 2026 note, the authors reported that with firm-by-time fixed effects the relative decline in exposed occupations among young workers became evident in 2024. They observed that earlier weakness could partly reflect other factors. In the August revision of their working paper, they estimated that by June 2026, employment of workers aged 22–25 in the two most exposed occupational quintiles was about 19% below the level implied by growth in the other three quintiles for the same ages. The comparison is among occupations within one age group and does not attribute the 37.65-point developer age gap to AI. February research note · August working paper revision, §§2.2, 2.4 and 5

Fewer hires or more layoffs?

Both studies that track worker flows point to fewer hires. The November developer ZIP reports headcount changes by age. A developer-specific accounting would require hires and separations for the same age group, with layoff and discharge reasons recorded separately. In the Stanford–ADP working paper, Figure B.7 examines annual flows across occupations. After 2022, hiring and separation rates both fell. The hiring rate fell more sharply for 22–25-year-olds in the highest-exposure quintile than for their counterparts in the lowest. The separation rate fell at least as much in the highest-exposure group as in the lowest. The payroll data track workers leaving a firm and combine departure reasons. Developer ZIP · Stanford working paper, §2.4 and Figure B.7

Lee Tucker’s U.S. Census Bureau working paper reaches a similar result using public Quarterly Workforce Indicators (QWI). For ages 22–24 in the most AI-exposed industry-by-state quintile, the regression-adjusted count of new hires still employed at quarter-end was 12.7% lower on average over the four quarters through Q2 2025 than over the four corresponding reference quarters. In an accounting exercise, restoring the hiring path estimated from less-exposed groups brings early-2025 employment close to its pre-release level. Fewer separations partially offset the hiring decline. The QWI comparison pools occupations, and separations include voluntary and involuntary exits. Public QWI files · Tucker, Figures 7 and 11

The rate of involuntary departures rose in the broader U.S. information industry. In BLS Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS) data, the average monthly hiring rate for January–September went from 3.64% in 2022 to 2.91% in 2026, while the monthly layoffs-and-discharges rate rose from 1.16% to 1.82%. Each figure averages the same nine non-seasonally-adjusted months, and September 2026 is preliminary. These are establishment survey rates for all ages and roles in NAICS 51. Many developers work in other industries, including computer systems design. BLS September 2026 release, Tables 9 and 12 · BLS monthly rates CSV via FRED

California unemployment-insurance records also show pockets of job loss. The California Policy Lab’s June 2026 report finds that initial claims by college-educated workers in highly exposed occupations remained elevated through May relative to their pre-pandemic levels, while the statewide share of claims from highly exposed occupations showed no significant rise across all education levels combined. The records track benefit claims and measure AI exposure by prior occupation. California Policy Lab report · Public claims tables

Tracking the same CPS respondents month to month

The Census Bureau’s monthly Current Population Survey (CPS) can track a worker across adjacent interviews. We linked 69 official files from January 2021 through October 2026 to form 67 adjacent-month pairs. For both age groups, we counted people whose main job was wage-and-salary software development (Census occupation 1021). The table pools weighted person-month transitions. Periods are labeled by the second interview month, and the numbers in parentheses are unweighted events and eligible matched records. For ages 22–25, the pooled hiring proxy fell from 6.26% to 5.04%, driven by fewer reported changes of main employer as a share of matched developers. Entry from nonemployment rose from 2.53% to 3.10%. The pooled decline also appears when restricting both periods to February–September. The proxy fluctuates across years. For interviews ending in January–October 2026 it was 8.76% for ages 22–25, based on 18 events in 222 matched records. The young group had only four observed job-loss-to-unemployment transitions in the later period, including one temporary layoff. The zero in the first period means the sample captured zero such events. These counts are too small to establish an age-specific layoff trend or attribute the change in payroll headcount.

How to reproduce the estimates

  1. Link people. Read HRHHID, HRHHID2 and PULINENO as the household and person key. Within adjacent calendar months, keep records with HRMIS increasing by one from 1–3 or 5–7, unchanged sex (PESEX) and race (PTDTRACE), and age (PRTAGE) unchanged or up one year. The 4–8–4 design samples an address for four months, pauses eight months and returns for four. Use the revised Census files for January 2024–July 2025, which correct household IDs. The absence of an October 2025 survey leaves two adjacent-month comparisons missing. CPS linkage documentation · Census revision notes, items 24–26 · BLS shutdown documentation
  2. Measure observable entries. For matched workers in the destination month, require employment (PEMLR=1/2), occupation PTIO1OCD=1021 and wage-and-salary class PEIO1COW=1–5. Group by age and weight with that month’s PWCMPWGT. The numerator combines workers who were looking for work or out of the labor force one month earlier (PEMLR=4–7) and continuously employed workers who reported a different main employer (PUIODP1=2). Both use all matched destination developers as the denominator. Prior-month temporary layoffs (PEMLR=3) stay in the denominator and are excluded from the numerator because returning to work can be a recall. Missing employer answers stay in the denominator. The numerator counts only reported changes among those employed at both interviews.
  3. Measure job-loss transitions. Use the same occupation and wage-and-salary screen in the origin month and group and weight by that month. Count workers who are unemployed in the second interview (PEMLR=3/4) with PRUNTYPE=1/2, temporary layoff or other job loss. Code 3, completion of a temporary job, is tracked separately. Divide the event weights by the weights of all matched origin-month developers in the age group.

What the sample can support

The median monthly matched destination sample had 26 young developers (range 14–40) and 72 older developers (range 56–104). The same respondents can appear in multiple pairs. Among later-period developers employed at both interviews, the employer question lacked a usable answer in 74 of 1,130 young records and 227 of 3,327 older records. Published research finds that missing responses to this question can be selective. The ratios weight matched records using cross-sectional weights, so sampling error, movers and unequal follow-up still matter. A survey-design variance estimate would be needed for population comparisons. Fujita, Moscarini and Postel-Vinay, 2024 · BLS labor-force flow methodology

The hiring proxy captures two observable paths. A reported main-employer change can include switching which of two jobs is primary, and an entry from nonemployment can include returning to a past employer. A layoff followed by reemployment before the next interview, or by leaving the labor force, falls outside the observed job-loss-to-unemployment measure. The CPS check adds direct evidence for these specific transitions and leaves the overall hiring and involuntary separation rates unresolved.

Does the same-month comparison show a rebound in 2026?

The 8.76% above covers January–October 2026 and uses the cross-sectional weight. The comparison below uses January–September in both years, because the CPS did not collect data in October 2025. It retains the same hiring proxy and occupation filter and uses the second interview’s official adjacent-month weight, PWLGWGT. January links to December of the preceding year. The young group’s estimate rose. Its year-over-year interval includes zero, so this comparison does not establish a rebound. Against the mean of the January–September estimates for 2023–2025, 2026 was 5.05 points higher [0.22, 10.51]. That result is close to the threshold. A linearized household-cluster interval for the same comparison has a lower endpoint only about 0.005 points above zero. Compared with the same months of 2022, the difference is +3.46 points [−2.01, +9.38].

The 2026 age gap is clearer. Using the cross-sectional weight on the same matches puts it at 6.75 points [2.17, 11.94]. Giving each month equal weight with PWLGWGT puts it at 6.71 points [2.40, 11.36]. The young group’s year-over-year estimates under those alternatives are +4.31 and +4.11 points, respectively, and both intervals include zero. The interval for the change in the age gap also includes zero. The gap measures the two groups’ observed entry and employer transitions among matched employed developers.

Reproduction. For each age-year cell, sum the second interview’s PWLGWGT for observed entries and divide by the weight sum for all matched destination developers. For the equal-month check, average the nine monthly ratios. The main comparison uses 20,000 bootstrap samples of the 779 observed households, drawn with replacement using seed 242649. In each draw, a household’s records from both age groups and all its observed months in both years move together. Recalculate the rates and their differences within each draw and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. Apply the same joint resampling to all relevant years for the historical contrasts. Cameron and Miller on cluster inference

In 2026, there were 17 events in 204 matched person-months among ages 22–25, and 18 in 740 among ages 41–49. The 204 young records came from 101 person keys, 64 of which appeared more than once. The intervals treat the published weights as fixed and approximate sampling uncertainty at the household level. They omit the full CPS stratification and higher-level sampling structure. Employer-answer nonresponse and movers remain limits of the proxy. BLS CPS standard errors and confidence intervals

Can research separate AI adoption from the tech slowdown?

Software hiring demand was already cooling in 2022. Indeed’s seasonally adjusted U.S. software-development postings index fell from 233.82 at its 2022 peak on February 28 to 142.64 on November 29, the day before ChatGPT’s public release. That was a 39.0% decline. On November 13, 2026, the index was 81.71. The index tracks a seven-day average of active postings on Indeed, with February 1, 2020 set to 100. In a separate study of Lightcast ads, Westby and co-authors estimate a 14–15% relative decline after the release in developer vacancies asking for up to three years of experience versus those asking for four or more. This measures advertised demand by experience. The release date and postings series provide no firm-level adoption dates. Indeed index CSV via FRED · OpenAI release date · IZA Discussion Paper 18723

Industry controls account for some of the slowdown. Federal Reserve economists Leland Crane and Paul Soto use CPS and O*NET to ask how U.S. coding-intensive employment would have evolved if each industry had kept its existing share of coders. A slowdown remains within industries after accounting for their overall employment changes. The Stanford–ADP age–exposure pattern also remains when the authors exclude computer occupations or exclude their definition of tech industries. These comparisons address industry-wide shocks. Demand can also shift toward particular roles or experience levels within a firm or industry. Federal Reserve FEDS 2026-018, §5.2 · Stanford working paper, Table C.1

One study isolates an adoption effect for early enterprise Copilot. In a November 2026 working paper, Lindsey Raymond (MIT) and Eleanor Dillon (Microsoft Research) link GitHub license and contract records to Revelio Labs employment histories at 5,414 multinationals. Pre-scheduled software contract reviews made earlier Copilot purchases more likely, allowing a comparison with firms whose reviews had yet to occur in the same calendar period. For firms induced to adopt earlier by their reviews, the estimate four quarters after review was 29% fewer quarterly technical hires, with an estimated 40% decline for junior software engineers. The study finds no statistically detectable increase in technical separations. The causal interpretation depends on the scheduled reviews affecting employment through Copilot adoption. Pre-launch reviews serve as a placebo, and estimates are similar after allowing different trends by industry and firm size. Raymond and Dillon, §§2–4, 6 and Appendix Table A.14

The choice of comparison and outcome matters. A separate study by Matthew Baird and co-authors, using GitHub and LinkedIn records, finds that Copilot adoption is associated with a higher monthly probability of hiring a software engineer, driven by entry-level hires. Raymond and Dillon’s design uses the review schedule to address selection into early adoption, and their main results concern the number of hires at firms. They find no statistically detectable change in whether a firm makes any technical hires. Baird et al., Contemporary Economic Policy · Raymond and Dillon, Table 1

The Copilot evidence comes from established enterprise GitHub customers observed through June 2025 and defines junior by job seniority. The paper makes the estimates and robustness checks public and describes the underlying internal licensing and commercial employment records. The published evidence supports an additional effect of AI adoption on hiring in that setting. Quantifying AI’s share of the 22–25 developer decline nationwide would require adoption dates and employment flows by age, occupation and reason for leaving in a more representative sample.

The monthly estimates are volatile, with only 23–48 survey respondents per month for software developers aged 22–25. In a separate comparison using the American Community Survey, the authors estimated a −2.2 percentage-point gap between employment changes in the most and least exposed occupation quintiles for ages 22–25 over 2022–2024, with a confidence interval that includes zero. Teams trying to understand entry into the profession can track new hires, separations, the share of hires at junior levels and year-over-year headcount on a consistent basis. The cumulative gap records a divergence that has already happened. New hiring and employment flows can show whether it is accelerating.
