Time Management 01: Principles and Google Calendar Practice

PUBLISHED 2026.10.18 12 MIN READ — VIEWS #Time Management#Google Calendar#Google Tasks#GTD#Time Blocking#Personal Workflow

Part 1 of the Time Management series. This article establishes the foundation: choosing priorities, reserving time, and deciding between Google Calendar Events and Tasks. Product features were checked on October 18, 2026.

A full calendar can coexist with stalled work. A long task list can coexist with a last-minute scramble before a deadline. In both cases, the connection between commitments, available time, and concrete results needs attention.

For knowledge workers balancing design, collaboration, learning, and family life, a useful starting point is: choose work by importance, track deliverables with Tasks, reserve time in Calendar, and revise the plan through regular review.

1. Choose a method that addresses your problem

Methods serve different purposes: prioritization, capture, execution, and review. Start with the difficulty you actually face.

Method Core practice Useful when Common mistake
Eisenhower Matrix Consider importance and urgency together Too many requests compete for attention Treating every urgent request as important
GTD Capture, clarify, organize, reflect, engage Commitments are scattered across messages and memory Collecting items without defining next actions
Time blocking Reserve continuous time for work or an activity Design, writing, or learning gets interrupted Scheduling every available minute
Timeboxing Limit an effort and assess the result when time expires Research or polishing keeps expanding Marking unfinished work complete when the timer ends
Most important tasks Select one to three meaningful results each day Busy days produce little progress Choosing vague goals such as “work on the project”
Pomodoro Alternate focused sessions and breaks; 25 plus 5 minutes is a common starting point Starting or sustaining attention is difficult Mechanically interrupting productive deep work
Batching Handle similar small actions together Email, routine messages, approvals, or expenses fragment the day Delaying genuinely urgent issues
Weekly review Reconcile commitments, progress, and capacity Unfinished work keeps accumulating Moving dates without reconsidering commitments

The five GTD steps are described in David Allen's official guide. The situations and cautions above are practical applications, not promises of equal results for every person.

Importance and urgency in practice

Category Example Suggested response
Important and urgent Production incident or a critical delivery due soon Prioritize it and explicitly move displaced work
Important, less urgent Architecture improvement, skill development, health, family time Reserve time before other requests consume it
Urgent, less important Some ad hoc questions or repetitive follow-ups Negotiate timing, delegate, or batch
Lower importance and urgency Aimless browsing or repeatedly reorganizing tools Limit or remove

Importance depends on responsibilities, goals, and consequences. Deliberate rest and leisure have value even when they produce no work deliverable.

Time blocking versus timeboxing

Time blocking answers when to invest attention. Timeboxing limits how much to invest before reassessing.

  • A block: Tuesday, 09:00–10:30, reserved for architecture design.
  • A timebox: spend 60 minutes comparing three options, then record an initial conclusion and open questions.

Use them together. At the end, assess the actual result and decide whether to split the task, schedule more work, or reduce scope.

2. Practical golden rules

These are adjustable working principles. Suggested counts and percentages are starting points.

  1. Use one trusted capture entry point. Record commitments, then organize them. Capturing an idea does not make it today's obligation.
  2. Write tasks as a verb plus a deliverable. Replace “research MCP” with “compare three MCP authentication approaches in a one-page table.” Make the starting action and completion condition clear.
  3. Separate deadlines from execution time. A Friday submission requires earlier preparation, drafting, and revision. A deadline constrains delivery; a scheduled session provides capacity.
  4. Match demanding work to high-energy periods. Observe your own pattern and reserve your clearest hours for design, reasoning, or writing.
  5. Leave room for change. Initially plan around 60%–70% of discretionary time and adjust to actual interruptions. There is no universal optimum.
  6. Limit work in progress. Advance a few meaningful outcomes. When urgent work arrives, decide which earlier commitment moves.
  7. Learn from actual duration. Use major differences between estimates and reality to improve future planning.
  8. Allow deletion and renegotiation. Repeated postponement can signal a task that needs splitting, delegation, cancellation, or a revised commitment.

Two additional heuristics can help. The 80/20 idea encourages identifying high-value work without guaranteeing an exact ratio. The two-minute rule can clear tiny actions while processing your list; it should not turn every small interruption into an immediate context switch.

3. Give Calendar a clear role

Tool or object Responsibility Example
Tasks lists Finishable actions and unscheduled work Draft the proposal; book a health check
Calendar schedule Meetings, travel, personal activities, and execution time Review meeting; family trip; writing session
Documents or project tools Context, dependencies, complex plans, and decisions Architecture document; project milestones; research notes

Use the calendar to assess time commitments and capacity. Use task lists to track actions and completion.

Current Event and Task boundaries

Calendar Tasks connect with Google Tasks. Undated tasks can stay in a list; a date is required for calendar display. Current desktop documentation supports task start/end times and availability settings, while recurring tasks cannot have an end time. Duration alone therefore does not distinguish Tasks from Events. See Google's task documentation.

Dimension Task Event
Main purpose Track whether an action is completed Record an appointment or activity
Completion Can be marked complete No task-style completion checkbox
Time Can remain unscheduled or be scheduled A time range or all-day arrangement
Typical use Draft, book, submit, follow up Meet, travel, attend, celebrate
Participation Personal to-dos do not replace invitations Guests can be invited and respond

Events support invitations, attendance responses, and advance notifications. Guests using Google Calendar generally receive reminders according to their own settings. See Google's invitation and notification guide.

Calendar operating principles

Principle Suggested practice
Separate calendars by sharing needs Use work, personal, or shared-family calendars when useful; avoid one calendar per small project
Keep colors few and consistent Give colors stable meanings that make the week easy to scan
Plan weekly and execute daily Check overall capacity, then concentrate on today's commitments
Place fixed commitments before flexible work Add meetings and travel, then reserve time for key deliverables
Include preparation, travel, and wrap-up A physical appointment occupies more time than its meeting duration
Keep the all-day area readable Use it for dates, leave, or deadlines rather than every ordinary to-do
Align reminders with required action Account for meeting preparation or the time to leave home
Repeat stable routines Recurring classes and reviews fit better than indefinitely repeated temporary goals
Review sharing visibility Share availability or details according to need and sensitivity

For a Beijing-based routine, Asia/Shanghai is a useful primary time zone. Specify the intended zone for international meetings and check local display while traveling. See Google's time-zone guidance.

Reserving focused work

“Finish the options comparison” is a concrete outcome and can be a scheduled Task. “Study for two hours every week” is a recurring activity and can be a recurring Event. Usually, a Task that already reserves a time slot does not need an identical Event.

Eligible Workspace accounts can also use Focus time to mute Chat notifications and automatically decline meetings. Eligibility depends on the account and edition; an ordinary Event can still reserve time. See Google's Focus time documentation.

4. Detailed Event and Task examples

Ask whether you need to track an action's completion, record an activity, or coordinate attendance. A project can contain both objects when they represent different commitments.

Work and learning

Situation Suggested object Example and reason
Architecture review Event Wednesday 14:00–15:00, with agenda, document, and guests
Prepare review materials Task Finish the review draft; reserve 90 minutes beforehand
Technical research Task Compare three authentication options and produce a table
Regular study Recurring Event Saturday 09:00–10:00 to protect a regular activity
Specific learning outcome Task Read one chapter and write five notes
Follow up on feedback Task Ask for proposal feedback on Thursday
Weekly team meeting Recurring Event Monday 10:00–10:30 for shared attendance
Check backup results Recurring Task Check results and record exceptions each week
Submit final proposal Task with deadline Schedule drafting earlier and complete the actual submission
Shared delivery milestone Optional all-day Event Make the date visible; keep execution in specific tasks

“Waiting for feedback” is not an executable action. Define when and what to follow up on. Where a team already uses a project system, keep dependency tracking there.

Travel and personal life

Situation Suggested object Example and reason
Train or flight Event Record departure/arrival times and stations or airports
Buy tickets or check in Task Confirm each preparation action was completed
Travel to the station Optional Event Reserve the necessary transit time
Wedding anniversary Annual all-day Event Preserve an important date
Buy a gift or book a restaurant Task Track preparations before the date
Confirmed dinner Event Record the agreed time and place
Teachers' Day Optional annual all-day Event Make the occasion visible
Send greetings to teachers Annual Task Check off the actual action after sending
Arrange a health check Task Contact the provider and confirm requirements
Confirmed health check Event Record appointment, location, and preparation
Verify monthly bills Recurring Task Confirm payment rather than treating a reminder as proof
Booked exercise class Event Attend an agreed session
Complete a workout this week Task Track the action and schedule it as needed

A complete proposal-review example

Time Object Commitment Completion or participation condition
Monday 09:00–10:30 Task Capture requirements and constraints A usable requirements/constraints list
Tuesday 09:00–11:00 Task Compare options and draft the proposal A reviewable document
Tuesday afternoon Task Send review materials Correct version delivered to participants
Wednesday 14:00–15:00 Event Architecture review meeting Attend and record decisions
Thursday 09:00–10:00 Task Revise the proposal Update decisions and action items
Friday deadline Task Submit the final proposal Complete the agreed delivery process

Revision and submission are separate actions. Alternatively, keep one task with a scheduled work session and a Friday deadline if that is easier to maintain.

5. Keep the system trustworthy with a small routine

Frequency Suggested time Action
Weekly 20–30 minutes Review the next two weeks, choose key outcomes, and check capacity
Start of day About 5 minutes Select one to three priorities and confirm workable time slots
End of day About 5 minutes Mark real completions and decide the next action for unfinished work

For delayed work, ask whether it remains worthwhile, needs splitting, lacks a prerequisite, or requires renegotiation. Avoid automatically moving everything to tomorrow.

Try this for two weeks. Check whether meaningful outcomes are more consistent, fewer commitments are missed, and maintenance remains light. Adjust the schedule and categories to fit actual work.
