Choosing HDDs for Family Media Backups: 4–5 TB, SMR/CMR, and 1 TB Transfers

PUBLISHED 2026.10.20 13 MIN READ — VIEWS #HDD#SSD#Backup#CMR#SMR#Home Lab

Family photos, home videos, and a carefully organized media library need capacity, recoverability, and a sensible total cost. For a drive that rarely travels and mainly serves as an offline backup, size and appearance can take a back seat. Sustained writing still matters: importing 1 TB for the first time is a different workload from occasionally copying a few movies.

For repeated transfers of hundreds of GB to 1 TB, I favor a specifically identified 3.5-inch CMR HDD in a powered USB enclosure. For an initial archive followed by occasional additions, a ready-made portable HDD can still offer good value. Irreplaceable family files always need independent copies.

This article follows a selection process from initial shortlist to reassessment. The original criteria were ¥800–2,000, at least 4 TB, little concern about size, and a preference for HDDs and JD self-operated sellers. After examining Expansion write-performance reports, the final criteria became ¥700–1,500, 4–5 TB, excluding Seagate Expansion. The brand scope is Seagate, Western Digital, and Toshiba as HDD manufacturers; it is not a ranking of SSD or overall storage brands.

Research and price-reference date: 20 October 2026. This is a public-source buying guide, not my own hardware benchmark. Public listing prices, comparison-site information, JD self-operated offers, and checkout prices are distinguished. Stock, discounts, warranty, and seller identity must be checked on the actual offer.

1. Does Expansion become slow when writing around 1 TB?

Reports exist; a universal 1 TB threshold is unverified

Seagate explains that an external drive may initially show a high burst rate and then slow once its cache fills. SMR housekeeping can also increase sustained-write costs, but caching, recording technology, USB problems, and hardware faults are different explanations. Seagate performance guidance

An Expansion Portable 5 TB owner reports substantially slower sustained writes near full capacity. Conversely, an STKM5000400 purchaser reported roughly 100–125 MB/s sequential transfers around half capacity without encountering a sudden performance cliff. These are observations under particular conditions, not a guarantee for every production batch. 5 TB owner report · Original STKM5000400 review

The defensible conclusions are:

  • Long, large write operations can be slow.
  • It is unverified that every currently sold Expansion 5 TB must fall to a particular speed during a sequential 1 TB write to an empty drive.
  • First writes to an empty disk, overwrites near full capacity, small-file batches, and repeated backup updates are different tests.
  • Writing approximately 1 TB of data is not the same question as evaluating a 1 TB-capacity Expansion. Results from one desktop Expansion model also cannot describe the entire family.

Expansion is excluded here as a purchasing constraint, not as a finding that all Seagate drives are slow or unreliable. The useful filters are exact model, CMR/SMR, workload, sales channel, and backup copies.

A different brand does not automatically remove the issue

WD explicitly states that external-drive internals can vary, including recording technology, model, rotation speed, and cache. CMR or SMR is not uniformly guaranteed. A brand, capacity, or family name alone cannot establish sustained-write behavior. WD external-drive policy

Likewise, without model-specific evidence, a Toshiba 4 TB portable should not be presented as a confirmed way to avoid SMR. This guide evaluates these portable products with SMR risk in mind and does not promise that a brand swap solves TB-scale write slowdowns.

4. SMR versus CMR: mainly a write-behavior distinction

**CMR—Conventional Magnetic Recording—**places tracks beside one another. **SMR—Shingled Magnetic Recording—**partially overlaps tracks to increase density. Updating some SMR data can require reading, reorganizing, and rewriting a larger region. WD explanation · Toshiba technical paper

Workload or question CMR SMR
Frequent overwrites and random writes Usually more consistent Housekeeping can introduce substantial latency and variability
Large-file archives Suitable Can suit infrequent sequential writes if waiting is acceptable
Reading movies and photos Suitable Also suitable; a write slowdown does not automatically imply playback trouble
NAS, array rebuilds, databases Generally preferred, subject to compatibility Ordinary consumer models are generally not the first choice
Quality and lifetime Not a guarantee against failure Recording method alone does not establish poor quality

PMR—Perpendicular Magnetic Recording—is not a direct substitute for the CMR/SMR distinction. A PMR label alone is insufficient proof of CMR; check the full model and manufacturer specification.

SMR is not categorically unsuitable for cold backups. However, when sustained-write behavior is a specific purchasing concern, selecting a documented CMR model addresses that concern more directly than swapping logos. Seagate likewise distinguishes recording technologies by product family rather than assigning one technology to its entire brand. Seagate CMR/SMR list

5. HDD versus SSD: why HDD remains the preference here

Aspect HDD SSD
Cost per TB Usually lower for bulk media Usually higher at equivalent capacity
Sequential playback and archives Suitable; depends on model and workload Faster, though static media may not benefit much
Applications, editing, small files Slow random access Lower latency and better random performance
Transport and noise Moving parts, vulnerable to shocks while operating, audible mechanics No moving parts, better suited to travel, no mechanical noise
Extended power-off storage Economical but still needs checks and migration Retention depends on wear, temperature, and other conditions; not maintenance-free

See the manufacturer's explanation of HDD and SSD operation. There is no universal rule that every SSD loses its data after a fixed number of unpowered months. Wear and storage temperature matter. Retention guidance

HDD matches this capacity-focused budget; SSD is more useful for active work and frequent transport. Neither is an unattended ten-year vault. USB-C describes a connector, and an interface's theoretical bandwidth is not the drive's sustained-write speed.

6. Preserve family media by designing copies first

Separate irreplaceable memories from replaceable media

Irreplaceable files: family photos, home videos, and important documents deserve a 3-2-1 approach: three copies, two media types, and one off-site copy. An example is an original on a computer or home server, an offline HDD backup, and an encrypted off-site cloud copy or another remotely stored copy. Multiple HDDs improve device and location isolation but do not strictly provide two media types. Backup and 3-2-1 explanation

Replaceable media: decide how many copies movies and music deserve according to the cost of obtaining them again. Preserve catalogs and organization work without assuming every replaceable file needs three copies.

Give the active library and offline backup different roles

A practical starting point is an always-available home machine managing the active library, an external HDD connected periodically for backup and verification, and an off-site copy for important files. Safely eject and disconnect the backup after completion. A mobile work computer should not host the only continuously required service.

If services run in a Linux VM, do not keep the only data copy inside a disposable VM system disk. A separate virtual data disk helps separate data from system rebuilds, but when both disks share the host's physical SSD they share that physical failure domain. Attached external storage also does not automatically become an independently available NAS.

A drive continuously attached for playback is an active library. A disconnected drive updated on a schedule serves the offline backup role. RAID, synchronization, Kubernetes PVCs, and separate directories do not automatically provide independent backups. Synchronization can propagate deletion, making version retention or other recoverable copies important.

Verify contents and restoration

  • Verify file integrity after each backup and open sample photos and videos.
  • As a maintenance recommendation, perform a full read/integrity check every 6–12 months and test selected restores. This is not a universal manufacturer lifetime standard.
  • Merely powering a disk on does not repair or refresh every file. Normal SMART status is not a recovery guarantee.
  • Store disconnected disks somewhere dry, temperature-stable, and protected from impact. Prioritize protecting readable copies when errors or checksum mismatches appear.
  • Plan migration as capacity, device age, and interfaces change. Retain the older copy until copying, verification, and restoration checks succeed.

With a ¥1,500 total budget, buy one disk as an additional backup and retain the source files. At the A5 reference of ¥1,199, two 4 TB units would cost about ¥2,398. A complete multi-disk setup does not fit that budget merely because it is desirable. Moving everything to the new disk and deleting the originals still leaves one copy.
